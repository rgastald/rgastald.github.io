About

I am a Ph.D. student in applied mathematics at CERMICS, École des Ponts (ENPC), and a member of the MATHERIALS project-team at Inria Paris. My thesis, Efficient computation of stochastic dynamics out of equilibrium, is supervised by Gabriel Stoltz and Urbain Vaes.

My Ph.D. is funded by a collaborative CNRS / Imperial College London project, in collaboration with Grigorios A. Pavliotis.

My work sits at the interface of analysis, probability and numerical methods, on problems arising in statistical physics. I am interested in quantities that are expensive to estimate by direct simulation — transport coefficients and rare event probabilities — and in the mathematical analysis that explains why, and by how much, a given estimator can be improved.

Research

Transport coefficients and dynamical reweighting

Transport coefficients — mobility, thermal conductivity, self-diffusion — measure the linear response of a molecular system to a small external perturbation. They can be estimated either from a nonequilibrium dynamics, extrapolating to a vanishing forcing, or through Green–Kubo formulas that express them as integrated time correlations at equilibrium. Both routes suffer from a poor signal-to-noise ratio: the quantity of interest is a small response buried in thermal fluctuations. I study importance sampling strategies based on Girsanov's theorem, which reweight trajectories of the reference dynamics onto those of a modified one, together with the asymptotic analysis of the variance of the resulting estimators.

Rare events and adaptive multilevel splitting

Reaction rates and metastable transition probabilities are governed by events so unlikely that direct Monte Carlo simulation never observes them. Adaptive Multilevel Splitting (AMS) replaces a single rare event by a sequence of likely ones: replicas are simulated in parallel, the least advanced along a reaction coordinate — a score function — are discarded, and the survivors are branched. How efficient the method is depends critically on how well that score approximates the committor of the dynamics. I work on this dependence, on the variance of the resulting estimators, and on combining AMS with importance sampling — in particular on the initial condition — so that the method remains reliable when the score is imperfect.

Mean-field limits of interacting particle systems

A system of weakly interacting particles converges, as the number of particles grows, to a nonlinear McKean–Vlasov equation. For kinetic (underdamped) dynamics one may also send the friction to infinity, which yields an overdamped, diffusive description. With G. A. Pavliotis, G. Stoltz and U. Vaes, I study the regime in which these two limits are taken jointly, for a kinetic weakly interacting particle system.

Convergence to equilibrium and hypocoercivity

The Fokker–Planck equations attached to kinetic dynamics are degenerate: dissipation acts on the velocities only, and the generator is neither symmetric nor coercive. Hypocoercive techniques nonetheless give quantitative exponential rates of convergence to equilibrium. Such estimates are the analytical backbone of the error and variance analyses above, and I use them both in the study of mean-field models and in the analysis of sampling algorithms.

The numerical experiments in my work are implemented in Julia.

Earlier work

Before starting my Ph.D., I worked with A. Henrot and I. Lucardesi on the Blaschke–Santaló diagram of area, perimeter and moment of inertia — a question in convex geometry and shape optimization, describing which triples of these three quantities are attainable by planar convex bodies.
